Comparing medicines that never met

Most new medicines are tested against one comparator, but payers and HTA bodies want to know how they compare with everything else in use. When no trial put the two side by side, analysts build an indirect treatment comparison. This page works through the family of methods, from the tempting shortcut that should never be used to the population-adjusted methods that HTA bodies now scrutinise most closely.

The running example

Drug Z was tested against Chemo A in the ZEN-1 trial. Immunotherapy B was tested against Chemo A in B-301. Nobody has run Drug Z against Immunotherapy B, but a country’s PICO asks for exactly that comparison (see Project 08).

The Bucher indirect comparison

Bucher and colleagues (1997) showed that if two trials share a comparator, the effect of Drug Z against Immunotherapy B can be estimated from the two effects against Chemo A, keeping each trial’s randomisation intact. On the log scale the effects subtract and their variances add.

Adding a small head-to-head trial closes a loop

Suppose a small trial, ZB-2, did compare Drug Z with Immunotherapy B. Now there is direct evidence and indirect evidence through Chemo A. A network meta-analysis combines both, weighting each by its precision, and the closed loop lets us test whether they agree. ZEN-1 and B-301 keep the values set in Part 2.

Summarised from the HTA Coordination Group’s methodological guideline on direct and indirect comparisons (2024) and NICE DSU TSD18. Anchored comparisons should always be preferred to unanchored ones.

Three ideas to take away

Keep the randomisation

An anchored comparison only compares like with like: each medicine against the shared comparator, inside its own trial. Differences in prognosis between trials then cancel out. A naive comparison of single arms throws that protection away.

Effect modifiers are the real threat

Anchored methods survive differences in prognosis but not differences in factors that change how well a medicine works. Population-adjusted methods exist for that case, but they cost precision and depend on knowing which factors matter.

Uncertainty only grows

Variances add in every indirect step, and reweighting shrinks the effective sample. An indirect estimate is almost always less certain than the trials behind it, and an honest report says so.
