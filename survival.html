From survival curve to cost-effectiveness

Almost every access decision for a cancer medicine rests on three questions. How much longer do patients live? What happens after the trial stops following them? Is the extra survival worth the price? These three hands-on tools walk through each one with a hypothetical medicine, Drug Y, and simulated data you can change and re-run.

How to use it
  • Part 1 shows how to read a Kaplan–Meier curve, a median and a hazard ratio.
  • Part 2 fits the standard survival models to three years of data and shows how far apart their long-term predictions land.
  • Part 3 turns survival into life-years, QALYs and costs, and compares the result with published thresholds.

All trials, patients and prices are simulated. Drug Y does not exist.

The curve

A Kaplan–Meier curve starts at 100% and steps down each time a patient dies. Patients still alive when last seen are censored: they count as “at risk” until then, and are then removed, shown as ticks. The curve’s tail rests on few patients, so it is the least reliable part.

The median

Median survival is the time at which the curve crosses 50%. If fewer than half the patients have died by the data cut-off, the median is not reached (NR). Medians describe one point on the curve, not the whole curve.

The hazard ratio

The hazard ratio compares the risk of death at any moment among patients still alive. An HR of 0.70 means about 30% lower risk on Drug Y, at every point in time, if hazards are proportional. The same reading applies to progression-free survival, where the event is progression or death.

Models are fitted to the simulated patient-level data by maximum likelihood, the way an analyst would. The NICE DSU list also includes the generalised gamma, not shown here.

Why the tails split

Each model makes a different assumption about how the risk of death changes over time: constant (exponential), steadily rising or falling (Weibull, Gompertz), or rising then falling (log-normal, log-logistic). They can all fit three years of data well and still disagree by years about the mean.

Why the mean matters

Cost-effectiveness models use mean survival, the area under the whole curve, not the median. Most of the difference between models sits in the extrapolated tail, so the model choice can drive the ICER more than the trial result does.

How analysts choose

NICE DSU guidance asks analysts to fit all the standard models and compare them with statistical fit (AIC, BIC) and diagnostic plots. Crucially, it also asks them to check the tails for clinical plausibility against external data and expert opinion, since good fit to observed data says little about the tail.

Partitioned survival

The two survival curves split the cohort into three states. Below the PFS curve: progression-free. Between PFS and OS: progressed. Above OS: dead. Each month, the time spent in each state collects costs and quality of life.

QALYs and the ICER

A QALY is a year of life weighted by its quality (1 = full health). The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio is the extra cost divided by the extra QALYs. Agencies compare it with a threshold or reference value; above it, the price usually has to come down.

Thresholds differ

England uses a range and a severity weighting; the Netherlands sets reference values by burden of disease; Ireland, Japan and ICER in the US each use their own figures and for different purposes. The comparison panel shows the price at which Drug Y would meet each one.
